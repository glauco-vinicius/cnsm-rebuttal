# Response to Reviewers — Paper #174
## "Evaluating Open-Weight LLMs for Hyperledger Fabric Chaincode Security: Complementarity, Coverage, and Limitations"

We thank the reviewers for their careful, detailed reading of the manuscript and for the constructive comments that follow. We are especially grateful that two of the three reviewers recommend acceptance, and we take particular encouragement from Reviewer 1's assessment that, despite scoring the paper as Weak Reject, it is nonetheless "a useful paper" that they "would like to see published in some form" — a judgment we read as reflecting confidence in the value of the underlying empirical study rather than a rejection of its contribution. We are likewise grateful to Reviewer 2 for rating the paper Highly Relevant and recommending Weak Accept, and to Reviewer 3 for recommending acceptance outright. Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 3's engagement with our limitations discussion — on single-run evaluation, corpus composition, and the candidate-pool validation design — helped us see exactly where the manuscript needed to be more explicit rather than merely more defensive, and we address each point below.

We respond to each reviewer in turn, following the order of their comments. Where a comment required us to re-examine our own execution logs, we report exactly what we found, including one correction to the manuscript's Table V footnote and one clarification that required no correction at all.

## Response to Reviewer 1

We thank the reviewer for the close reading that surfaced the Table III/IV vs. Table V inconsistency around category V-05 (Comment 1) — this was the single most consequential comment we received, and addressing it properly required us to go back to our raw execution logs and the HFCCT tool's own source code, which in turn allowed us to resolve the question with primary evidence rather than assumption.

**Comment 1 (V-05 contradiction).** The reviewer correctly identifies that Tables III and IV state that HFCCT does not implement detection for category V-05 (Reified Object Addresses), while Section IV-C attributes three false positives to a "V-05 detector." We confirm that HFCCT's output for our corpus genuinely included three warnings labeled V-05, emitted by the tool's own `check_re_addr()` routine. This routine is registered in the tool's dispatch list and executes unconditionally, but it is not part of the 15-category detection scope documented in the tool's original publication [Li et al., 2022] — Table 2 of that paper lists 15 detection methods and explicitly excludes Reified Object Addresses and Cross Channel Chaincode Invocation. We additionally verified this against HFCCT's public repository directly: the entire codebase was published as a single, unmodified commit in July 2021, roughly eleven months before the tool's own publication, ruling out the possibility that a different tool version explains the discrepancy. We have therefore corrected Table III to reflect this detector as *undocumented* rather than *not implemented*, and revised the corresponding sentence in Section IV-B accordingly. In the course of this verification we also found that the three chaincode names listed in the Table V footnote (atb-basic, atb-queries, token-utxo) do not match our own execution logs, which instead point to cp-digibank, cp-magneto, and token-utxo. We have traced this to a transcription error made when the footnote was originally assembled and have corrected it; the finding counts and Precision figures reported in Section IV-C are unaffected by this correction.

**Comment 2 (undescribed automated pre-filter agent).** We agree that the pre-filter agent's identity and prompting strategy should not be left implicit. The automated pre-filter is **[MODEL NAME — to be confirmed before submission]**, prompted to classify each candidate finding for verdict correctness, taxonomy category, code-location accuracy, and whether the reported reasoning is supported by cited source evidence. We agree that using an unvalidated model as a first validation layer is a limitation that deserves to be confronted directly, and we have added this specification to Section III-E. We note that the GLM-5.2 case — where the pre-filter's automated estimate (~45%) diverged from the subsequent single-reviewer estimate (~51%) — offers a useful, if limited, check on pre-filter calibration, which we now discuss explicitly in Section IV-A, and we list full pre-filter-versus-human agreement measurement as a priority item under Future Work.

**Comment 3 (reasoning correctness oversold).** We agree that the original framing overstated what the study delivers. We have revised the contribution statement in Sections II and III to describe two quantitative metrics — Response Rate and Precision — plus one qualitative observation on reasoning faithfulness (the `json.Unmarshal` inconsistency reported in Section IV-A), rather than presenting these as three independently measured dimensions. A systematic rationale-evidence consistency rate computed over the full validated finding set is a natural extension of this observation, which we now list explicitly under Future Work.

**Comment 4 (statistical uncertainty on Precision comparisons).** We thank the reviewer for this suggestion, which led us to compute 95% Wilson score confidence intervals for Precision for all five evaluated models, using $n = \mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}$ consistent with the Precision definition already stated in Section III-F:

| Model | TP/(TP+FP) | Point estimate | Wilson 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| GLM-5.2 | 26/51 | 51.0% | [37.7%, 64.1%] |
| MiMo-V2.5-Pro | 7/11 | 63.6% | [35.4%, 84.8%] |
| Kimi-K2.6 | 8/16 | 50.0% | [28.0%, 72.0%] |
| Step-3.7-Flash | 11/18 | 61.1% | [38.6%, 79.7%] |
| DeepSeek-V4-Flash | 0/19 | 0.0% | [0.0%, 16.8%] |

The intervals for MiMo-V2.5-Pro and GLM-5.2 overlap substantially given the small sample size for MiMo ($n=11$), and we agree the point-estimate ranking does not support a strong claim of relative model superiority. We have revised Sections IV-A and V accordingly: the sentence previously stating that "MiMo-V2.5-Pro achieved the highest Precision" now reads that MiMo-V2.5-Pro exhibited the *highest point estimate*, but that the difference from GLM-5.2 is not statistically conclusive at this sample size, with intervals reported inline in the revised Table VI. We note that the one statistically distinguishable result at this sample size is DeepSeek-V4-Flash, whose interval ([0%, 16.8%]) does not overlap with any other model's, which supports rather than undermines our claim of a systematic domain-knowledge gap specific to that model. We have also moved the CCSE/z3-solver limitation for HFCCT out of the Table V footnote and into the main text of Section IV-C, stating explicitly that HFCCT's observed coverage in our evaluation may understate its full capability.

**Comment 5 (corpus is pedagogical).** We agree, and have added an explicit statement to the Limitations paragraph (Section V) noting that the fabric-samples chaincodes are pedagogical/tutorial artifacts and do not represent production chaincode complexity or adversarial conditions. We have also softened the framing of the auction/EndAuction finding: rather than describing it as "the most consequential confirmed finding," we now describe it as a confirmed true positive illustrating a realistic non-determinism pattern, observed in a single tutorial repository.

**Comments 6–9 (minor points).** We thank the reviewer for these observations, all of which we have incorporated: the abstract's category list now distinguishes confirmed-TP categories (V-4, V-15, V-16, V-17) from candidate-only categories, consistent with Table IV; Section IV-A has been retitled to align with RQ1's framing; a clarifying sentence has been added at the first use of "coverage" in Section III-F to state explicitly that it denotes candidate-generation coverage rather than recall; and Section III-E now clarifies that outside-scope findings are treated identically in the automated pre-filter and manual estimates, grounding the ~45%/~51% comparison for GLM-5.2 discussed under Comment 2.

**Comment 10 (fit with the CNSM/network-management audience).** We appreciate this framing suggestion and agree it strengthens the paper's fit for this venue. We have revised the introduction and related work (Section II) to position chaincode assurance explicitly as a security-management concern for permissioned-ledger services operating within managed enterprise networks, consistent with the CI/CD security-gate framing already present in Figure 1. We note that the TNSM/CNSM community has itself framed blockchain security as squarely within network- and service-management scope, citing the *IEEE Transactions on Network and Service Management* guest editorial on "Recent Advances on Blockchain for Network and Service Management" [Kanhere et al., 2022], and we connect our shift-left gate design to recent work on blockchain-aware CI/CD security [Saleh et al., ENASE 2025]. We have revised the closing paragraph of Section II accordingly.

## Response to Reviewer 2

We thank the reviewer for rating the paper Highly Relevant and for a set of comments that substantially sharpen the manuscript's honesty about its own limitations.

**Single-run evaluation / inference variability.** We agree this is an important caveat. We have moved the discussion of temperature-0 residual non-determinism [Yuan et al., NeurIPS 2025] from a passing mention to an explicit, prominently placed limitation in Section V, and we list multi-run stability analysis as the top priority for future work.

**Single-reviewer Precision validation.** We agree this deserves more prominence than a limitations footnote. We have moved this caveat to the opening of Section IV, before per-model Precision results are presented, explicitly framing all reported Precision figures as single-reviewer estimates pending dual-reviewer validation with Cohen's κ, now the first item under Future Work.

**Cause of repository-level failures.** We thank the reviewer for raising this. On review, we found the distinction the reviewer asks for is already present in the submitted manuscript: Section IV-A attributes MiMo-V2.5-Pro's four full-repository failures to provider-level errors and Kimi-K2.6's single full-repository failure (the `irs` repository) to a pipeline-level failure. In both cases the failure count reconciles exactly with 17 (the number of agents per repository) multiplied by the number of affected repositories — 68 and 17 invocations respectively — confirming these are clean, fully-attributed full-repository wipeouts rather than partial or ambiguous failures. We have added a sentence to Section IV-A making this attribution more explicit and pointing out the exact reconciliation, so the distinction is not left implicit for the reader. We note this is a separate phenomenon from Step-3.7-Flash's lower Response Rate, which arises from partial per-repository agent failures rather than full-repository wipeouts, and is discussed separately in the same section.

**Data contamination.** We agree this is a relevant threat to validity that we did not sufficiently discuss. We have added a paragraph to Section V acknowledging that the corpus consists of publicly available repositories that may have been present in model pretraining data, and that confirmed findings may in some cases reflect memorization rather than genuine vulnerability reasoning over unseen code. As a concrete future direction, our research group intends to explore constructing a contamination-free evaluation corpus from proprietary, industry-deployed chaincode repositories that have never been publicly released. After sanitizing such repositories to remove business-sensitive content, one option we are considering is deliberately injecting known vulnerability patterns aligned with the taxonomy of Li et al., which would also provide ground-truth labels enabling a proper recall measurement — something the current candidate pool cannot support. We view this as a promising direction to address both the contamination and recall limitations identified in this evaluation.

## Response to Reviewer 3

We thank the reviewer for the positive assessment and for recommending acceptance. The concerns raised — single-run execution and a small, homogeneous corpus — overlap with points raised by Reviewers 1 and 2 above: we have elevated single-run variability and corpus-composition caveats to prominent, explicit limitations in Section V, and clarified throughout that the candidate pool is not exhaustive ground truth and that recall is not derived from it (Section III-F). We further note that we will address these limitations at the source in near-term follow-on work: running multi-run execution to quantify residual inference non-determinism, and expanding the corpus beyond fabric-samples toward a more diverse set of production-representative chaincodes. We have listed both directions explicitly under Future Work rather than treating them as open questions with no concrete path forward.

We believe the revisions above directly address every substantive concern raised, and we would welcome the opportunity to incorporate them fully in a camera-ready revision.
